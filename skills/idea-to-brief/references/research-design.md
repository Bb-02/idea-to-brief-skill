# Research design by purpose

Read this when choosing or applying methods for a practical investigation or deep research. In task design, encode useful methods for the future executor without running the substantive research. In direct research, apply them to the requested investigation and deliver findings. Use only the parts that help; this reference is not a mandatory process, questionnaire, or visible plan.

## Find the useful question

Start with what the user wants to decide, understand, or do now; keep possible future use secondary unless the user makes it the assignment. Translate the topic into questions whose answers affect that purpose. Preserve personal observations without treating them as established population-level claims. For each material assumption, consider what observation would support it and what would change the proposed direction; do not present your proposed explanation or bias diagnosis as a finding.

For example, “find theories to market a planner” may suggest two questions: who experiences a problem the product can solve, and what evidence supports the promised benefit? Make a material interpretation visible in the task description or answer rather than declaring a hidden goal or withholding useful work until it is approved. Do not turn an illustrative habit duration into a scientific premise or the product's only positioning.

Add a missing angle when you can explain how it might change the answer. Prefer the smallest addition with practical value. Present broader adjacent questions as optional scope choices.

## Match methods to the work

| Purpose | Useful design choices | What makes the result useful |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discover options or inspiration | Explore distinct use cases and source communities; vary search terms beyond the user's examples; check existence, fit, and duplicates. | A varied, usable selection that explains why each item belongs. Exhaustive coverage is only needed when requested. |
| Compare options for a decision | Identify decisive constraints and tradeoffs; collect evidence for the same questions across candidates; investigate unknowns that could reverse the choice. | Conditional recommendations, with unsupported comparisons left unresolved. No forced universal winner. |
| Diagnose or assess a proposed technical change | Inspect project requirements, implementation, and measurements; separate observed symptoms from possible causes; propose the smallest useful test of competing explanations. | A supported diagnosis or a concrete next measurement, plus options that preserve agreed requirements. General documentation does not establish this project's bottleneck. |
| Learn or develop judgment | Start with a focused explanation and informative comparisons; distinguish candidate mechanisms from established findings and note conditions that matter. | The user understands the question better and can identify useful implications. Formal design principles or a comprehensive literature review require a corresponding need. |
| Explore feasible ideas | Diversify mechanisms or user situations, then screen against resources and goals; separate evidence about precedents from original proposals; identify cheap tests of the strongest uncertainties. | Candidates with a concrete experience, practical limits, and a next validation step. Creativity does not require fictitious proof of demand. |

A task may combine purposes. Keep their roles clear: a broad search can discover candidates before a focused comparison; a diagnostic investigation can precede development. Do not automatically run every method for every candidate.

## Calibrate the scope

For exploratory curiosity, a concise explanation with selected evidence and a few relevant implications may be enough. Do not automatically combine every related discipline, venue type, theory, and application into the assignment. Leave budgets and future project conditions unknown when they do not affect the immediate question; use conditional implications where relevant. Judge scope by the requested use, not by how many sections could be written about the topic.

- **Simple search:** specify coverage axes that add variety, source families, basic screening, deduplication, and a compact output. “Excellent solo projects” may call for varied platforms and uses, project homepages and creator accounts, checks on who made it, and a reason it is worth studying. It does not automatically need a business teardown of every item.
- **Practical investigation:** design for use. For promotion methods, ask for suitable products, places to reach people, material to prepare, concrete steps and example messages, follow-up, effort, and signals for continuing or stopping. Separate different product types when the action differs; keep case experience distinct from recommendations.
- **Deep research:** give important questions independent attention and reconcile findings. For game engines, separate game types and target platforms, editor and AI coding workflows, asset production, testing, distribution, costs, and ecosystem evidence as relevant. Verify small-team and AI-use claims separately, study failures as well as successes, and make conditional recommendations with cheap prototype checks.

Do not bind all these dimensions to one intensity setting. Broad coverage can use shallow checks; an important narrow decision can justify deep verification. More research resources should support useful evidence and alternatives, not more stages or documents. Keep research capacity distinct from development or purchase spending; an unspecified budget does not establish willingness to pay for tools, assets, or services.

## Build an evidence strategy

Identify which claims matter most and what evidence could answer them. For product capabilities, inspect current primary documentation. For scientific effects, seek appropriate research and examine its limits. For project behavior, use actual project evidence. For lived experience, use firsthand reports while keeping them separate from population-level claims.

Use the user's examples as starting points and search alternative terms, categories, or explanations when coverage matters. Early results should reveal whether the search is too narrow, which directions are duplicates, and which gaps deserve attention. Popularity alone is not quality, and several pages repeating one source are not independent corroboration.

Separate a factual uncertainty that more searching could resolve from a choice that belongs to the user. Do not disguise that choice as an objective research conclusion. When evidence remains thin, narrow the claim or leave it unresolved.

In direct research, inspect the sources used to support important claims and cite them near the relevant findings. An attractive search snippet is a lead, not evidence for a detailed conclusion that was never checked. Distinguish evidence quality from whether it supports or challenges a hypothesis; a counterexample is not itself a quality grade. When using a proxy for the actual outcome, explain what it can and cannot establish. Do not infer a negative outcome solely from missing publicity or unavailable records.

## Allocate depth and stop

Spend more effort where uncertainty could materially change the result, not evenly across every heading. Important competing explanations or counterexamples deserve investigation; minor details may need only a straightforward source check. If the user excludes a method, use an allowed approach or report the resulting limitation.

Stop a branch when it meets the agreed criterion, repeats known material, or is blocked by unavailable evidence with no useful alternative. Stop the task when the deliverable supports the user's intended use to the agreed depth. Report significant remaining uncertainty instead of manufacturing closure.

## Adapt methods and resources only where useful

For a substantial assignment, adapt search terms, candidate priorities, and verification effort from findings; in task design, describe how the executor should do so. Define what answers or deliverables are sufficient, and surface material scope changes. Do not require approval for routine search decisions or an internal plan.

If deep work benefits from parallel inquiry and the host permits it, split by independent questions and reconcile assumptions, evidence, and duplication centrally. More roles do not automatically add independent evidence. Preserve explicit current global concurrency limits, including nested agents; otherwise use host capacity and observed resource pressure rather than inventing a number. Use bounded retries for temporary errors and alternatives for persistent failures. Simple tasks can omit these operational details entirely. Never claim a delegated inquiry, source check, or experiment happened unless it actually did.
